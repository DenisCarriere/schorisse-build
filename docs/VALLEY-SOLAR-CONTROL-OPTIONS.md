# Valley glass solar-control options

Status: **Option C selected — Rev 09 geometry review pending**

The owner selected Option C for design development. It is now integrated in the
Rev 09 review model with a compact permeable patio, but it is not yet approved
geometry. Rev 08 remains the last approved photoreal authority.

The model inputs are in [`models/solar-control-options.json`](../models/solar-control-options.json).
Each fixed-camera study is generated from the approved 17.0 × 7.5 m model; none
is permitted to move a wall, opening, room, stair or mezzanine.

## Shared design rules

- Keep the rectangular ground-floor facade fully glazed from floor to the
  3.0 m transom.
- Keep all fixed screens above the ground-floor vision zone; roof projections
  remain overhead.
- Preserve the full valley view from seated and standing eye levels.
- Use slender matte-black metal coordinated with the window surround and roof.
- Retain the hedge, neighbouring brick building, paved passage, lawn and valley
  outlook.
- Treat the external shading as the first solar-control layer. Final glazing
  still requires an appropriate visible-light transmission and solar-factor
  specification from the facade engineer.

## Option A — deep roof visor

Extend both black-tiled roof planes 1.60 m beyond the valley glass.

**Strengths**

- closest to the simple Belgian-barn language;
- no screen or fin crosses any glazing;
- maximum daylight and completely unobstructed valley view;
- passive, visually quiet and mechanically simple.

**Limits**

- primarily controls high summer sun;
- does less against low morning/evening sun and glare;
- the long cantilever and wind uplift require structural design.

## Option B — floating brise-soleil

Use a 1.00 m roof projection plus a 1.25 m-deep open blade canopy immediately
above the 3.0 m transom. Six slender blades run outward from the facade with
open sky between them.

**Strengths**

- stronger high-angle shade than the shorter roof projection alone;
- open blades still admit diffuse daylight and preserve views below;
- the transom gives the canopy a natural structural datum;
- reads as a precise black line rather than a heavy porch.

**Limits**

- requires concealed drainage, thermal-break and support details;
- blade pitch must be tuned to the surveyed facade azimuth and actual sun path;
- still needs a glare strategy for very low sun.

## Option C — upper-gable veil

Use a 1.25 m roof projection and nine slender operable vertical fins over only
the triangular upper glazing. The rectangular ground floor remains completely
clear.

**Strengths**

- adjustable response to lower-angle sun;
- protects the upper double-height volume, where heat can accumulate;
- preserves the uninterrupted ground-floor panorama;
- creates a distinctive contemporary lantern character.

**Limits**

- most visible departure from the restrained barn form;
- motors, controls, wind sensors and maintenance add complexity;
- upper-only fins cannot solve all glare through the ground-floor glass.

### Selected Rev 09 development

- extend the roof visor to 1.25 m as the fixed high-sun layer;
- keep exactly nine motorised fins above the 3.0 m transom;
- use temperature, solar-irradiance and wind-safe control with manual override;
- reserve a concealed, fully retractable exterior screen at the ground-floor
  transom only if PHPP/designPH shows that low sun still causes overheating;
- thermally isolate every motor, bracket, box and fixing from the ICF envelope;
- add a compact water-permeable patio using open-jointed local stone/clay
  pavers or compacted local gravel, with two small chairs and one coffee table;
- keep the hedge and the neighbour-side paved passage completely clear.

The retractable ground-floor screen is not shown as a permanent facade layer.
If required, it should use an open-weave, view-through exterior fabric and
disappear into a thermally separated head box when not deployed.

## Passive House precedent review

Passive House Institute guidance supports a layered approach rather than fins
as decoration:

- external movable shading is substantially more effective than internal
  shading and can retain outward visibility with perforated or translucent
  screens;
- for south-oriented room-height glazing in a Central European reference case,
  a horizontal projection around 1.25 m materially reduced overheating without
  noticeably increasing annual heating demand;
- low solar-factor glass reduces gains all year and should therefore be selected
  only after project-specific summer-comfort calculation;
- summer bypass/night purge ventilation remains important alongside shading;
- shading boxes and metal supports can create meaningful installation thermal
  bridges and must be included in the facade detail and energy model.

Relevant examples include the first Darmstadt-Kranichstein Passive Houses,
which use motorised external blinds and night ventilation; the monitored Good
Energy House in Minneapolis, which uses automated external blinds on east,
west and south windows; and the Hanssen-Höppener Passive House, where a deep
projecting upper floor/recessed glazing provides fixed shade without an active
system.

## Information required before sizing

These dimensions compare architectural directions; they are not a solar or
energy calculation. Design development needs:

1. measured facade azimuth and horizon/terrain profile;
2. seasonal hourly shadow study, including the neighbouring building and hedge;
3. glass area, visible-light transmission, solar factor, U-value and frame data;
4. room cooling target, internal gains and ventilation strategy;
5. structural checks for cantilever, snow, wind and water management;
6. bird-safe glass and external-screen access/maintenance review.
